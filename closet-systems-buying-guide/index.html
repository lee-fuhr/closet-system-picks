How to choose a closet systems

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

Start by measuring your space and thinking about how you actually use your closet, what you hang, fold, or need to access daily. Focus on adjustable systems with sturdy materials like coated wire or laminate, and choose a brand (Elfa, ClosetMaid, Rubbermaid) that fits your budget and DIY comfort level. The best system is the one you can install correctly and will actually keep organized.

What measurements do I need before shopping?

Grab a tape measure and a notepad. You need the width, height, and depth of your closet space, measure at multiple points because walls are rarely perfectly straight. Also note the location of door frames, light switches, and any obstacles like baseboard heaters. Don’t forget to measure the depth of the hanging space you have available. A standard rod is around 12 to 16 inches from the back wall, but if your closet is shallow, you may need a system that mounts rod brackets closer to the wall. Write down these numbers before you look at any product: they’ll save you from buying shelves that won’t fit.

Should I choose wire shelves, laminate, or solid wood?

Wire shelving (like ClosetMaid’s basic line) is budget-friendly, easy to cut to length, and lets air circulate so clothes don’t get musty. The downside: small items can fall through the gaps, and it’s not as sturdy for heavy sweater piles. Laminate (melamine) systems, such as those from Rubbermaid or Easy Track, give you a smooth, solid surface that looks more like built-in furniture. They’re stronger than wire and easier to clean, but they’re heavier to install and can chip if you drop something on a corner. Solid wood is premium, think California Closets, beautiful and durable, but costs considerably more and often requires professional installation.

What are the key parts of a modular closet system?

Most systems start with wall-mounted standards (vertical tracks) that hold brackets for shelves and rods. You’ll then choose shelves, hanging rods, and optional add-ons like drawers, shoe racks, and tie/belt racks. The flexibility comes from the bracket spacing, you can move shelves up or down without drilling new holes as long as the standard is in place. Drawers are great for folded items you want out of sight, but they consume depth. Two-level hanging rods (one above the other) double your hanging space but only work if your closet is at least 50 to 60 inches tall. Baskets and bins on wire shelves can corral accessories, but don’t overbuy extras before you know what you actually need.

Can I install a closet system myself, or should I hire someone?

Many modular systems are designed for a confident DIYer with basic tools: a drill, level, stud finder, and maybe a saw if your shelves aren’t pre-cut. Brands like ClosetMaid and Rubbermaid offer detailed instructions and online videos. The biggest challenge is finding studs and making sure the standards are plumb and level, a crooked system will wobble and frustrate you later. If you have an unusually shaped closet, a very high ceiling, or you simply hate home improvement projects, hiring a pro (or using a brand’s white-glove service) may be worth the cost. Expect to pay a premium, but you’ll save your back and your patience. For most people, a weekend of careful work will get a basic system installed just fine.

How important is the adjustability of shelves and rods?

Extremely important. A good modular system lets you reposition shelves and rods every 1 to 2 inches along the standard. This matters because your wardrobe will change, you might switch from long coats to folded t-shirts, or you may buy a new pair of boots that need a taller cubby. If the system uses fixed holes or preset heights, you’re stuck. Look for heavy-duty metal standards with slots that allow for tool-free adjustment (some brands have a simple lever or pin system). The ability to add an extra rod for kids’ clothes later, or to convert a shelf area into a hanging section, makes the system much more versatile than a one-size-fits-all wire shelf.

What should I know about closet organizers for shoes?

Shoe storage is one of the most common pain points. Modular systems offer tilt-out shelves, wire cascading racks, or simple flat shelves. The best solution depends on your collection size and your willingness to see them. Tilt-out bins hide shoes behind a door but limit how many you can fit. Open wire racks let air circulate but can dent delicate heels. A pragmatic approach: use standard adjustable shelves set at the right height (about 6 to 8 inches for flats, 10 to 12 for boots) and add a slim shoe rack or organizer that sits on the shelf itself. That way, if your shoe collection changes, you just move the shelf, not the whole system. Avoid designs that require you to stack shoes in a tight cubby, you’ll never take the bottom pair out.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a modular closet system typically cost?

Prices vary widely by material and brand. A basic wire system for a small reach-in closet can be very budget-friendly (a few hundred dollars), while a full laminate setup for a walk-in might cost in the low thousands. High-end wood systems from custom brands can run several thousand. Shop sales and compare kits versus individual parts.

Can I mix and match different brands’ parts?

Generally no. Each brand uses its own standard and bracket design, so components are not interchangeable. Stick with one system from start to finish. However, you can often add third-party accessories like hooks or baskets that attach to the shelves themselves, as long as they fit the shelf dimensions.

Do I need to cut the shelves myself?

It depends on the product. Many wire shelves can be cut with a hacksaw or snips to fit your exact width. Laminate shelves often come in standard lengths and may require cutting with a circular saw (or you can order custom lengths from some brands). Pre-cut kits are available for common closet sizes if you want to avoid cutting altogether.

Will a modular closet system work in a rental apartment?

Yes, but with caution. Some systems require screwing standards into studs, which leaves holes when you move out. If you’re a tenant, look for freestanding units or tension-rod systems that don’t attach to walls. You can also use removable adhesive strips for lightweight add-ons, but they won’t support heavy loads. Talk to your landlord before drilling.

How long does installation usually take?

For a standard reach-in closet (about 6–8 feet wide), a DIY install can take a few hours to a full day, depending on your skill level. A walk-in closet might take a weekend. Professional installers typically finish a reach-in in two to four hours and a walk-in in a single day. Factor extra time if you need to paint or patch walls first.

What’s the best way to maintain a modular closet system?

Dust shelves regularly, especially if you use open wire, because dust settles on everything. For laminate, wipe with a damp cloth and avoid harsh cleaners that can dull the finish. Wood systems may need occasional polishing. Check the tightness of screws and bracket connections every six months, as vibrations from daily use can loosen them.

In shortChoosing a modular closet system comes down to measuring honestly, picking a material that matches your taste and tolerance for installation, and leaving room for future changes. Don’t get dazzled by too many add-ons you won’t use. A well-planned, simple system that you actually install correctly will serve you better than a complicated one that sits in boxes. Start with your basics, rods and shelves, then add drawers and extras only after you’ve lived with the setup for a month.
